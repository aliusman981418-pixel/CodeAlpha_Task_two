PAGE 2 • SPOT THE CLUES

Look beyond the logo.

A message can look official and still be a trap. Look for a combination of clues, then verify through a trusted route.

01

Urgency and fear

Threats such as “your account will close in 10 minutes” try to make you act before thinking.

“Immediate action required — access will be suspended!”
02

Unexpected links

A displayed link can hide a different destination. On a computer, hover to preview it; on mobile, avoid opening unexpected links.

The message says “company login” but the address is unfamiliar.
03

Sender mismatch

Check the complete email address and domain, not only the display name. Watch for misspellings and extra words.

A “Help Desk” message from an unrelated public email domain.
04

Unexpected files

Unexpected attachments or requests to enable macros can deliver malware or lead to credential theft.

“Open this invoice now” when you were not expecting an invoice.
05

Secret or payment requests

Do not share passwords, one-time codes, recovery codes, or confidential information because a message asks.

A supposed manager requests an urgent gift-card purchase.
06

Odd context

Unexpected login alerts, invoices, delivery problems, or requests that break normal procedures deserve extra checks.

A supplier suddenly changes bank details by email.
TRAINING EXAMPLE • NOT A REAL EMAIL
FromIT Support <support@example.invalid>
SubjectURGENT: Mailbox will be disabled

Your account will be disabled shortly. Use the link below and enter your password and one-time code to keep access.

[Verify your account]

What should make you pause?

  • Pressure to act immediately.
  • A request for a password and one-time code.
  • An unfamiliar sender domain and an unverified link.

This example uses the reserved example.invalid domain and does not link to a real website.

A SAFE RESPONSE

Stop. Check. Confirm.

  1. Stop: Do not click, reply, download, pay, or share a code while you are unsure.
  2. Check: Inspect the full sender address and website domain. Do not rely on logos, caller ID, or the name shown in a message.
  3. Confirm: Visit the official site by typing its known address yourself, or contact the person using a number or channel you already trust.
  4. Report: Use your organization's approved phishing-report process or contact its IT/security team.
  5. Recover: If you entered credentials, go to the genuine website, change the password, revoke suspicious sessions if possible, and contact support. If you shared a one-time code or payment details, tell the relevant provider immediately.
!

Social engineering can happen anywhere.

Attackers may impersonate a friend, teacher, employer, delivery company, bank, or IT support. A request can be convincing and still need independent verification.
